Common causes:
1. Interrupted or incomplete package installation/update — if install.packages("cli") (or an update that pulled in cli as a dependency) got interrupted, killed, or ran out of disk space mid-write, the .rdb file can be left in a broken/truncated state.
2. Version/architecture mismatch — the path shows 4.5-arm64. If cli was ever installed under a different R version or architecture, the lazy-load database can become unreadable.
3. Filesystem/sync issues — if your R library folder lives somewhere touched by iCloud, Dropbox, Time Machine, or a permissions change mid-write, files can get corrupted or partially synced.
4. Manual copying of library folders between machines or R versions instead of reinstalling packages properly.

How to fix it:
remove.packages("cli")
install.packages("cli")

If that command itself errors, delete the package folder directly and reinstall:
rm -rf "/Library/Frameworks/R.framework/Versions/4.5-arm64/Resources/library/cli"
then in R:
install.packages("cli")

If it happens again / to multiple packages, that's a sign of a deeper problem — check disk health, make sure nothing is syncing your R library folder, and consider whether packages got installed under mismatched R versions/architectures.

USER:
is stats sa's core inflation measure CPI excl food and NAB and fuel or CPI ex food and NAB, fuel, energy

ASSISTANT:
It's CPI excluding food and NAB, fuel, and energy — the "and" plus "and" phrasing is the standard, i.e. core CPI strips out three groupings: food & non-alcoholic beverages (NAB), fuel, and energy (electricity/other energy). Core inflation, typically excluding volatile items such as food, non-alcoholic beverages, fuel, and energy, provides a clearer signal of underlying, persistent inflationary pressures, and the SARB closely monitors both.

This is consistently how StatsSA/SARB define it in their reporting — core inflation excludes the volatile food and non-alcoholic beverages, fuel and energy categories, and the annual core inflation rate excludes food, non-alcoholic beverages, fuel, and energy.

So "energy" isn't a separate add-on beyond fuel in a different sense — it typically refers to things like electricity (administered energy prices), while "fuel" refers to petrol/diesel/transport fuel. Both are excluded, distinct from each other, alongside food & NAB. So your second phrasing — CPI ex food and NAB, fuel, energy — is the more complete and accurate one.

when was the last change in weights

ASSISTANT:

Key details:
- Stats SA updated the CPI basket of goods and services and respective weights in the January 2025 CPI statistical release, published in February 2025, based mainly on the Income and Expenditure Survey (IES) results.

USER:
can that not be done by geom_hline()

ASSISTANT:
No — geom_hline() draws a horizontal line across the entire width of the panel; it takes a yintercept and always extends to the full x-axis range. There's no argument to make it stop partway through since it doesn't know about the x variable at all.

Two real options:
1. geom_segment() — the correct tool, letting you specify x-start/x-end and y-start/y-end.
2. annotate("segment", ...) — functionally identical, doesn't need its own data frame:

ggplot(plot1_df, aes(x = date_col)) +
  geom_line(aes(y = headline_yoy, colour = "headline")) +
  annotate("segment", x = min(plot1_df$date_col), xend = as.Date("2025-11-01"),
           y = 4.5, yend = 4.5, linetype = "dotted", colour = sarb_palette["grid"], linewidth = 0.6) +

           y = 3, yend = 3, linetype = "dotted", colour = sarb_palette["grid"], linewidth = 0.6) +

USER:
do date-formatted columns automatically pick up the previous day with lag = 1

ASSISTANT:
No — lag(n = 1) operates on row position, not actual date values. It grabs whatever is in the row immediately above, regardless of what date that row represents.

If your data has a gap (missing day, weekend, holiday), lag(x, n = 1) returns whatever the previous row contains, not necessarily "yesterday."

Check with:
df %>% mutate(gap = date_col - lag(date_col)) %>% count(gap)

For a true calendar-day match:
df_lagged <- df %>%
  mutate(join_date = date_col - 1) %>%
  left_join(df %>% select(date_col, value_yesterday = value), 
            by = c("join_date" = "date_col"))

Bottom line: lag() is positional — only equivalent to "previous calendar day" if there are zero gaps. Fine for monthly CPI data; needs more care for daily FX data.

USER:
does lag n = 1 return row 2 when in row 1 or vice versa

ASSISTANT:
lag(x, n = 1) shifts values forward — at row 2, lag() gives you row 1's value. Not the reverse.

| Row | date | value | lag(value, 1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Jan | 10 | NA |
| 2 | Feb | 12 | 10 |
| 3 | Mar | 15 | 12 |

Rule: at row i, lag(x, n=1)[i] equals x[i-1]. First n rows are always NA.

This is why value - lag(value, 12) works correctly for YoY calculations — subtracting last year's value from this row's value.

The opposite operation is lead(): lead(x, n=1)[i] equals x[i+1].

USER:
do BER inflation expectations' t0 estimates from respondents represent the entire year inclduing previous months or only the remainder of the year

ASSISTANT:
Based on the BER's own methodology, the answer is: it's the full calendar-year average, including months already passed.

Key evidence: respondents have to indicate what they expect CPI inflation to be during the specific year — not whether they expect price increases to be up, the same, or down compared to the same time a year ago. The question is framed around the full-year outcome.

This is confirmed by how results are reported — e.g. Q2 2026 survey showing 4.4% (up from 3.6% in Q1) is described as "the annual average consumer inflation rate for this year," and later compared against the actual full-year outturn.

Practical implication: t0 estimates from a Q2/Q3 survey round already implicitly incorporate actual CPI prints from earlier months blended with expectations for remaining months — not a clean forward-only projection. Worth keeping in mind when comparing t0 forecasts across quarters within the same year.
